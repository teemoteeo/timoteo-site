The Non-Ownership of Data

In May 2023, for a digital-products law exam at the University of Milan, I wrote an essay called The non-ownership of data. The argument was simple: what we produce online stops being ours the moment it is recorded. Platforms collect it, keep it, process it and sell access to it. The GDPR was the first real attempt to give some of that control back. Three years later I tested it. I asked Spotify, Instagram and Google for everything they had on me, merged it into one timeline of 605,610 events over eleven years, and asked the question the essay only asked in theory: how much can someone work out about a person from their traces alone?

These traces, once aggregated, are used by third parties to pursue their own interests.

— my essay, Milan, 2023

01Explicit and Implicit Datathe map of where my phone pointed

The essay separated two kinds of data. Explicit data is what you hand over yourself: name, birthday, email. Implicit data is what gets recorded while they watch what you do: every song, like, search, map view and message timestamp. My explicit data would fit on a business card. This is the implicit part, month by month.

02What Can Be Inferredeleven conclusions, one of them wrong

In the essay, profiling was something platforms did to us. Here I did it to myself, with Python and a few evenings. Every line below comes from the data alone.

03You Are the ProductMeta's own profile of me

If you're not paying for it, you're not the customer, you are the product being sold.

— quoted in my essay, Milan, 2023

I quoted that line in 2023. The Instagram export shows what it looks like from the inside. Meta doesn't only keep my activity. It also keeps the list of companies it has sold me to, and the labels it used to do it.

    04Other People's DataCambridge Analytica, at home

    The part of the essay I think about most is Cambridge Analytica. The quiz didn't only collect the data of whoever took it. It collected their friends' data too, and 87 million people ended up in a dataset they never agreed to. My own export has the same problem, on a small scale.

    84,220messages in my Instagram export were written by other people, across 811 conversations. None of those people asked for a copy of their words to end up in my zip file, and none of them could stop it.

    That is why no name of anyone else appears on this page, and why the analysis only uses the shape of conversations: when, how often, with how many people. Never what was said. That shape is revealing, and it can also be wrong: see the relationship line in section 02.

    06Did I Choose This?me or the feed

    The essay's biggest worry wasn't privacy. It was soft power: feeds decide what we see, what we see shapes what we think, and what we think feeds the algorithm again. My data shows my taste changing a lot. Italian rap goes from almost nothing to a third of my listening, and YouTube drifts toward streamers, podcasts and "money" content. So I checked who actually picked each video and each song: me, or the feed.

    07Where the Data Lieserrors a naive reading would make

    A profile doesn't have to be true to be used. These are the places where the data would have fooled me if I hadn't lived it.

    my attention span didn't collapseSpotify's skip rate jumps from 0% to 58% between 2021 and 2023. My listening didn't change, the field did: before 2022 the export doesn't record skips at all. A naive analysis would have written a story about TikTok.
    I lived in ParisSpotify's connection country puts me in Italy for almost all of 2025, while Maps puts me in Paris. The reason is roaming: an Italian SIM gets an Italian IP.
    looking is not beingA map view means I looked at a place, not that I was there. Cities only count when they dominate a month.
    gaps look like silence2015–2016 is almost empty because my first phone and old accounts logged little, not because I was offline. Missing data is missing, not zero.

    08The Right I Wrote Aboutthe GDPR in practice

    In 2023 I listed the rights the GDPR introduced. This project exists because of two of them. It also shows where they fall short.

    art. 15 · accessAnyone can ask any company what it holds about them. It worked: three requests, three exports, gigabytes in total. No lawyer needed.
    art. 20 · portabilityThe law asks for a "structured, commonly used and machine-readable format". In practice I got JSON, CSV, HTML pages and an mbox. Instagram's JSON even garbles Italian accents (più becomes piÃ¹). Machine-readable, but only with some work.
    what the export doesn't sayI got back the inputs: what I did. In my exports, only Meta's included any of the outputs, meaning the labels and audiences, and only a small part of them. What they're actually used for stays on their side, and that was the essay's point about accountability.
    fines as a cost of doing businessIn 2023 Meta was fined €1.2 billion over transfers of EU data to the US. That's a lot of money, but a small share of its revenue. The fines are real; whether they change anything is less clear.

    09Considerationswhat's left

    It isn't only about writing better laws or building safer technology. Above all it's about building a society that is more aware and more responsible.

    — my essay, Milan, 2023

    In 2023 I ended the essay optimistic, but without an answer. Three years later I still don't have one. What I have is this page: the same argument, made with my own data instead of other people's scandals. If you want to see yours, ask for it: Google Takeout, Spotify (ask for the extended streaming history), and Instagram under Accounts Center → Your information and permissions. It takes from a few hours to 30 days.
